01 — YOUR LOGO, PUT TO WORK
THE NAME — ONE SPELLING FIX

Your logo stays exactly as it is. On the Arabic, though, the hamza moves seat depending on the grammatical case, so the same word is spelled three ways — and only one of them is right for a title standing on its own.

دواؤك
Use this as the app name
Nominative — the case a title stands in alone. Hamza sits on the wāw.
دواءك
Correct after a verb
Accusative. اعرف دواءك — “know your medicine”. Your spelling, right where it belongs: the tagline.
دوائك
Correct after a preposition
Genitive. عن دوائك — “about your medicine”. Not a title form.

So the pair reads Medication Identifier · دواؤك in the store and under the icon, with اعرف دواءك as the tagline — which is also the line that tells a new user what the app does. If you would rather run one spelling everywhere, make the Arabic name the full phrase اعرف دواءك: correct as you wrote it, and more memorable than a single word.

WHY IT IS BUILT THIS WAY
1
The logo sits on paper, not on colour
A white-ground icon needs a light bar behind it. Beside it is the app name in full — the only place it appears at length.
2
The viewfinder is already running
No Take a photo button, no mode picker. The brackets frame the pack and the dashed box says what to aim at: the printed name.
3
One instruction, in plain words
“Point at the front of the box.” The second line covers the two cases people actually hit — a strip, or a bottle label.
4
Gallery and flash flank the shutter
Upload stops being a separate mode and becomes what it is: one small button, for a photo you already took.
5
Typing is one tap, not a fork
Bad light, a torn box, shaky hands — there is a full-width field waiting, and it opens the same search.
6
The privacy line is a feature
Photos stay on your phone. No account. Two facts that answer a first-time user before they raise the objection.

TURN 2

New mark, honest critique, and the app I'd ship

Three logo directions (2a / 2b / 2c) — all built on the pack and its printed name, none on a pill, for the reason set out below — then eight things I'd change about the current build, then six screens showing what those changes look like. Type is IBM Plex Sans + Plex Sans Arabic — one superfamily so English and Arabic finally match.

01 — THE MARK
WHAT'S WRONG WITH IT

Four ideas fight in one 48px square: a bottle, an Rx, wifi waves and a tick. Rx is an American pharmacy convention that means nothing to an Arabic-speaking user, and the wifi waves say "smart device", not "identify". The white circle floating on green is the Android adaptive-icon fallback — it throws away a third of the canvas and makes the artwork tiny on the home screen. At thumbnail size in Play search it turns to soup.
THE CATEGORY TRAP — WHY NO PILL

Every app on the shelf next to yours is a pill identifier: you hold up a loose tablet and it guesses from shape, colour and the imprint code. That is a database-matching product, and their icons are all capsules and tablets because a capsule is literally the input.

Yours is not that app. Your backend asks for drugName and strength — it reads the printed name off a box, a strip or a label, and typing the name works just as well. So a capsule in the icon would promise a feature you don't have, and bury you among a dozen identical pills in search. The pack and its label are the honest subject — and nobody in the category has claimed them.

02 — WHAT I'D CHANGE
01
The home screen asks a question before it does anything

Photo, upload and type are three equal boxes, and the one real button ships disabled. Someone standing in a pharmacy holding a box has to make a filing decision first.

→ Open straight into the camera. Typing is one tap away, not a fork in the road.
02
Nothing checks that the scan was right

A photo goes in and a drug page comes out. In a medication app a silent mis-read isn't a bug, it's a safety incident — and the user has no way to catch it.

→ A confirm step: here's what we read, here's the match, is this your box?
03
The disclaimer is the loudest thing in the app

Five lines of red-titled legal text repeat on every screen, in 12px grey that fails contrast. Users banner-blind it inside a day, so it protects nobody.

→ Accept it once on first run; after that one quiet line where it's relevant.
04
Export buttons sit above the drug's name

Download Word, Print, Save to List — three chips that wrap onto two rows before you've been told what the medicine is. Nobody exports a document they haven't read.

→ Answer first. Save and share live in one persistent bar at the bottom.
05
Five tabs hide the answer behind four taps

Use / Dosage / Food / Side Effects / Precautions all render as filled pills that wrap to three rows at phone width, so the selected one doesn't look selected.

→ One scroll, ordered by what people actually ask, with the tabs demoted to jump links.
06
A warning looks exactly like a description

Every block is a white card, 16px radius, same shadow, same teal circle icon. "Common use" and "Do not take with" carry identical visual weight. Card soup.

→ Three tiers: plain facts on paper, amber for caution, red-bordered for never-do.
07
Arabic is a dropdown, not a first language

The globe menu sits third in the header for an audience that is largely Arabic-first, and the layout never mirrors. Half your users are the translated version of the app.

→ Follow the phone's locale, mirror the whole layout, one visible ع / EN toggle.
08
"My Medications" is a bookmark folder

A saved list with a delete icon gives no reason to reopen the app. Yet the moment two drugs sit in it, you know something worth telling the user.

→ Shelves per person, times of day, and an interaction check across the list.

Two smaller ones: Download Word should be a share sheet — nobody wants a .docx on a phone — and the body copy needs a floor of 16px with real contrast, because this audience is older eyes reading a box under pharmacy lighting. The one screen I'd keep almost as-is is "That's not a medication"; it's the most human thing in the app.
